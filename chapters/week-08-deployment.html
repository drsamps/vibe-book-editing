Deployment: Publishing, and Undoing a Bad Publish — Vibe Programming, Session 8

Adapted and simplified from the second half of Scott Murff’s chapter Version Control, Environments, and Deploys, in his textbook Intro to Product Management. The ideas are his. The wording and the business examples were written for these readings.

Reading time: about 20 minutes. Class opens with a short quiz on this reading.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

  1. Name the three environments your app runs in, and say what defines an environment.
  2. Find out which database each environment uses, and explain why that matters.
  3. Explain the difference between publishing a change and releasing it to users.
  4. Choose between a staging environment and a feature flag for a risky change.
  5. Roll back a bad publish, say what a rollback cannot undo, and find out what went wrong from the logs.

The big idea: more than one copy, and a way back

You have been publishing since the intro session. In session 3 you learned the gate: a pull request, a preview and a merge. After the merge, your hosting company publishes the change, and users have it.

Until now that has been safe, because nobody depended on your app. After session 7 it has staff, customers and orders. A bad change now reaches real people and real data.

Publishing a new version of an app is called a deployment. People say “deploy” as a verb.

Here is the main point of the session: your app runs as more than one copy. You try a change on a copy that cannot hurt anyone. You also keep a fast way back for when a bad change gets through.

Environments: three copies of your app

An environment is one running copy of your app, defined by which code it runs, where it runs and which settings it reads.

You already have three. You met two of them in session 2 and the third in session 3.

Environment Which code Where it runs Who sees it
Your computer Whatever you are working on, including changes you have not saved as a commit Your own computer, at an address starting with localhost Only you
Preview The latest commit on one branch or pull request The hosting company’s computers, at its own address Anyone you send the address to
Production main The hosting company’s computers, at your app’s real address Your users

Production is the proper name for what earlier sessions called the live app.

A theater works the same way. Actors rehearse in a practice room, hold a dress rehearsal on the real stage, and then perform for a paying audience. Nobody rehearses in front of the audience.

One difference between the copies will surprise you. The computer copy is put together a page at a time, as you open each page, and it tolerates some mistakes. For a preview or for production, the hosting company makes a build: the whole app assembled at once, the way it will actually run. Some mistakes appear only in the build. An app can work on your computer and still fail to publish.

That is why the build is one of the checks on a pull request, as you saw in session 5.

Rule of thumb: “it works on my computer” is a statement about one environment. Check the preview before you merge.

The settings decide the database

An environment uses whichever database its settings name, so a copy of the app is not automatically a copy of the data.

The settings an environment reads are a short list of names and values kept outside the code. Session 2 called the computer’s version the secrets file. The list includes the address of the database and the keys to reach it.

This leads to a fact that is easy to miss. The code and the database are separate choices. If your computer’s settings hold the production database’s address and keys, your computer is working on production data.

It is like a store’s cash register in training mode. The screens look identical to the real ones. Whether a sale you ring up is practice or real depends on which mode the register is in.

Worked example. You set up your project in the intro session with one database. Its address and keys went into your computer’s settings and into the hosting company’s settings for previews and production. All three environments share one database.

Now trace what happens.

  • A test order you place on your computer appears in front of real customers.
  • A reviewer who clicks around a preview of an unfinished feature changes real stock quantities.
  • The made-up seed data from session 6, loaded on your computer, lands in the live tables.

Your computer often holds the secret key too. Session 7 taught you that the secret key skips every policy. A mistake there reaches every user’s data.

Session 6 gave the rule. Once real people use your app, stop building against its database. Give the computer and the previews a database of their own.

Option What it is Good for
One database for everything Computer, previews and production share one Only until the first real user signs up
A second database A separate one with the same tables, filled with seed data Your computer and your previews
A database on your computer A copy that runs on your own computer Your computer and your tests. Previews cannot reach it.

The migrations from session 6 pay off here. Applying the same migrations to the second database gives it the same tables as production.

You can check your own setup in one request: “Which database does each of my environments use? Tell me the names only. Do not print any key.”

Rule of thumb: before you load test data or try an unfinished feature, know which database you are pointed at.

Staging: a rehearsal that matches the real thing

Staging is a copy of your app that closely matches production, where you try a change before any user sees it.

A staging environment has its own data and is not open to users. It is useful in proportion to how closely it matches production. It should be built the same way, hold the same settings with staging values, and have the same tables. Its database is separate.

Every difference from production is a place where “it worked on staging” may not hold in production. So keep the differences few, and write them down. Two differences are always needed.

  • Outgoing messages. A staging app must not email real customers. It sends only to your own team.
  • Money. Anything that charges a card runs in the payment service’s test mode.

A new restaurant rehearses this way with a soft opening. It serves a full evening of meals to friends and family before the public opening. The rehearsal is worth something only if the kitchen is set up the way it will be on opening night.

The simplest staging setup costs nothing extra. Point your previews at the second database from the last section. Then every pull request’s preview is a staging site for that one change.

What goes in the staging database? Seed data, or your own team’s accounts. Do not copy real customers’ personal data into staging. Session 7’s rule still applies: copying data somewhere is sending it there.

Rule of thumb: a staging environment with old tables or missing settings tells you little. Keep it matched, or do not trust it.

Publishing is not releasing

A change can be published to production and still be switched off, so that you choose who sees it and when.

So far, “published” and “available to users” have been the same moment. They do not have to be.

A feature flag is a setting the app checks before it shows new behavior. The new code is published to production for everyone. The flag decides, for each user, whether they get it. Turning the flag on for someone is called a release. It needs no new deployment. Turning it off stops the new behavior on the next request.

The example shop does this with a new seasonal flavor. Ice cream keeps for months frozen, so the pints can sit in the freezer for weeks before the flavor appears in the online catalog. Stocking it and listing it are separate decisions. The shop may show it to its regular customers first.

A flag can be kept in two ways.

Where the flag is kept What it allows
In an environment’s settings On or off for everyone in that environment, such as on in previews and off in production
In a table in your database On for you, then for twenty customers, then for everyone, by editing a row

Two cautions come with flags. First, check the flag on the server as well as on the screen. A hidden button over a working request can still be used, as you learned in sessions 2 and 7. Second, remove the flag once the feature is on for everyone. Forgotten flags are a kind of technical debt.

Staging or a flag?

They answer different questions.

Staging Feature flag
Where the new code runs A separate copy of the app Production
Whose data it touches Test data Real users’ data
Who sees it Your team The users the flag is on for
How you undo it Nothing reached production Turn the flag off

Ask two questions about the change.

  1. Does it take effect for everyone the moment it is published? A migration does. So does a changed setting. A flag cannot hide these, so try them on staging first.
  2. If the first people who get it hit the worst case, whose data is harmed, and can it be undone? If the harm is limited to your own team’s data, a flag is enough. Some changes could do something permanent to someone outside your team, such as emailing customers. Try those on staging first, then release them behind a flag.

Worked example. The shop wants a reorder suggestion on the stock screen, using each product’s reorder point. It needs a new column in the products table.

  1. Computer. The agent builds it on a branch, against your computer’s database, with the feature behind a flag.
  2. Preview on staging data. The migration is applied to the second database. You click through the preview. This is the first time the migration has run on tables shaped like production’s.
  3. Production, flag off. You apply the migration to production and merge. The code is published. Customers and staff see no change.
  4. Release. You turn the flag on for your own staff account, then for all staff.
  5. Clean up. A small pull request removes the flag.

Rule of thumb: staging protects other people’s data. A flag controls who sees a change. Many teams use both.

Rolling back a bad publish

When production is broken, go back to the last good version first, and find the cause afterward.

Fixing a problem while users are hitting it is slow and error-prone. Returning to the last good version takes seconds and lets you fix the cause calmly.

A production line has a cord any operator can pull to stop the line. Nobody is expected to diagnose the defect while bad units keep coming off the end. You stop first.

You have two ways back.

Way back What it does When to use it
Rollback at the hosting company Points your app’s address back at an earlier published version, in seconds. Your project’s files do not change. First, when production is broken
Revert (session 3) Makes a new commit that undoes the bad one. Merging it publishes the undo. Afterward, to make the undo permanent in your project

One detail catches people. After a rollback, the hosting company may stop publishing new merges automatically until you tell it to resume. Your fix can be merged and still not be live. Check after any rollback.

What a rollback cannot undo

Session 3 gave the limit, and it applies here in full. Every published version shares one production database. A rollback changes which code runs. It does not touch the rows.

Suppose a bad publish ran for ten minutes and marked forty orders as shipped. After the rollback, the old code runs against forty wrong rows.

The worst case is a migration that removes something. If the bad publish deleted a column, the old code now runs against a table it does not fit.

So migrations should add before they remove. Adding a column is safe, because old code ignores a column it does not use. Removing one deserves a backup first, and a run on staging.

Repairing the data afterward

The rollback stops the damage. Repairing the rows is a second, slower job.

  1. Find the rows. Ask the agent which rows the bad version changed, using the time window and the logs covered below.
  2. Plan the repair. In plan mode, have the agent propose how to put each row back. Read the plan, and ask how many rows it will touch, as in session 6.
  3. Restore if you must. If the damage is too wide to repair row by row, restore the database from a backup taken before the bad publish. Changes made since that backup are lost, so this is the last resort.

A restore needs a backup to exist. Database plans differ in whether they keep backups and how far back they go. Find out what yours keeps now, before you need it.

Rule of thumb: practice a rollback once on a harmless change, and check what backups you have. The first time you need either should not be the first time you look.

See what is happening: logs

You cannot fix what you cannot see, and most users who hit an error leave without telling you.

A log is a line of text the app writes when something happens. An error report is a record of a failure with its details, collected automatically.

Each of the three places from session 2 keeps its own record.

Where it ran Where to look What you find
Browser The Console and Network tabs from session 5 Errors on that one device
Server The hosting company’s logs Errors and messages from your server code
Database The database service’s logs Refused requests and failed sign-ins

The browser’s record is only on the device in front of you. To see what real users hit, an app can add an error reporting service. It collects every failure from every user, groups them and can email you.

A package leaves a record the same way. Each handoff is scanned. When a package goes missing, nobody guesses. They read the scan history and find the last place it was seen.

A useful log line says what happened, where, and an ID you can trace: “Ship order 2042, staff 7, refused: order is cancelled.” It must not hold personal data, passwords or tokens. Session 7’s rule applies, because logs are stored by your hosting company and can be read by anyone on the project.

One practical warning. On free plans, hosting companies keep server logs for only a short time. Look soon after a problem.

Rule of thumb: when a user reports a problem, get the time it happened. Then read the logs before you ask the agent to guess.

Key terms

Term Plain-English meaning
Deployment Publishing a new version of an app
Environment One running copy of the app: which code, where it runs and which settings it reads
Preview A copy of the app built from one branch or pull request, at its own address
Production The copy at your app’s real address, which your users use
Build The whole app assembled at once, the way it will actually run
Staging An environment kept as close to production as possible, with its own data, for trying a change
Feature flag A setting the app checks to decide which users get new behavior
Release Turning a published feature on for some or all users
Rollback Pointing the app’s address back at an earlier published version
Log A line of text the app writes when something happens
Error report A record of a failure with its details, collected automatically
