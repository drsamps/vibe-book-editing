Logins and Security: Who May Do What — Vibe Programming, Session 7

Adapted and simplified from parts of two of Scott Murff’s chapters, Anatomy of an Application and Data and APIs, in his textbook Intro to Product Management. The ideas are his. The wording and the business examples were written for these readings.

Reading time: about 20 minutes. Class opens with a short quiz on this reading.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

  1. Explain the difference between authentication and authorization.
  2. Describe what happens when a user signs in, and why you do not build sign-in yourself.
  3. State an access rule in plain English, say where it must be enforced, and test it with two accounts and a security review.
  4. Tell a public key from a secret key, and say what to do when a secret leaks.
  5. Decide what data may leave your database, including data sent to AI tools.

The big idea: so far, your app has trusted everyone

Until now the example shop has had no login. Everyone who opens it sees every customer, every order and every product, and can change them.

That was fine for a class project with made-up data. This session the shop gets two kinds of user. Staff run the shop. Customers can sign in to their own online portal, see what is in stock, place an order and follow their own orders. Products now also record the shop’s cost, what it paid for each item. A customer must not see the shop’s costs, its sales dashboard or another customer’s orders. Your own app needs the same kind of thinking: who uses it, and what each kind of user may see.

In session 2 you learned the trust line. The user controls the browser, so hiding something on a screen protects nothing. This session applies that idea to people.

Here is the main point of the session: signing in proves who someone is. It does not decide what they may do. That second decision must be enforced where the data lives.

Two questions: who are you, and what may you do?

Security in an app comes down to two separate questions, and confusing them is the most common mistake.

Question Name Answered
Who is this? Authentication Once, at sign-in
May this person do this, to this record? Authorization On every request

An office building handles visitors the same way. At the front desk, a guard checks your identification once and gives you a badge. That is authentication. After that, every door reads the badge and decides whether it opens for you. That is authorization. A contractor’s badge is real, and it still does not open the finance office.

The common mistake is to treat authorization as a screen problem. The agent hides the Edit button from customers and reports that customers cannot edit. But the button is in the browser, and so is the request it would have sent. Anyone can send that request by hand.

Worked example. The shop has a page for editing products and prices. Customers never see a link to it. A curious customer types the page’s address directly, and the page loads, because the only protection was the missing link.

Now add a rule in the database that lets only staff change the products table. The page may still load. But every attempt to save fails. That is the result you want. The screen is a courtesy. The rule is the protection.

Rule of thumb: when the agent says a user “cannot” do something, ask what stops them. If the answer is a hidden button, nothing stops them.

Signing in: let a service do it

Handling passwords safely is hard, and a sign-in service already does it. Do not build your own.

Sign-in looks simple. Underneath, someone must store passwords so that a thief who copies the database cannot read them. Someone must handle forgotten passwords, confirm email addresses and remember who is signed in. Each of these has been done wrong by serious companies.

The service that runs your database also offers sign-in. It can check an email and password. It can also let people sign in through another service they already trust, such as “Sign in with Google.” In that case the other service vouches for who the user is.

A company makes the same choice about its building. It does not design its own lock and badge system. It buys one from a firm that does nothing else, and spends its own effort deciding who gets which badge.

What happens at sign-in

  1. The user types an email and a password.
  2. The sign-in service checks them.
  3. If they are correct, it gives the browser a token: a long, signed string that says who the user is.
  4. The browser sends that token with every later request.
  5. The server and the database read the token to learn who is asking.

The token is your visitor badge. Three facts about it matter.

  • It cannot be edited. The service signs it, so a changed token is rejected.
  • It expires. A token lasts a short time and is renewed in the background while you stay signed in.
  • Whoever holds it is you. Anyone who copies your token can act as you until it expires.

That last fact gives you a rule. Never paste a token into a chat, an email, a support ticket or a prompt to an AI.

Rule of thumb: ask the agent to use the sign-in service that comes with your stack. If it starts writing its own password handling, stop it.

Access rules live in the database

An access rule belongs on the table it protects, so it holds no matter which screen or request asks.

Start by writing your rules in plain English, one line per role and table. This is a business decision, and it is yours to make.

Table Staff may A customer may
Products Read and change everything Read the name and price, but not the cost
Stock Read and change everything Read what is available, and where
Orders Read everything Create an order, and read only their own
Customers Read everything Read and change only their own details

The database can enforce rules like these row by row. A rule of this kind is called a policy. It is attached to a table, and it says which rows a request may touch, based on who is asking.

The clearest way to picture a policy is as a filter the database adds silently to every query. Take the rule “a customer may read only their own orders.” When J. Park asks for all orders, the database behaves as if the request were “all orders where the customer is J. Park.” Only J. Park’s rows come back, whatever the browser requested.

A policy chooses rows, not columns. It cannot hide one column, such as the cost, while showing the rest of the row. Asked to hide the cost, the agent will probably hide it on the screen. That is the mistake this reading warns against. The cost still arrives in the browser, where developer tools can show it. Hiding a column needs a different tool, such as keeping costs in a separate staff-only table. Ask the agent which tool it used.

A bank works this way. One system holds millions of accounts. Each customer can see and move money only in their own. That rule is enforced by the bank’s system on every transaction. It is not a sign on the door.

In the stack this course uses, this matters even more than usual. Session 2 mentioned that the browser can send requests straight to the database. When it does, there is no server in between to check anything. The database’s own rules are the only protection.

Two situations to recognize on sight

Situation What you see What it means
Rules are switched off for a table The app works perfectly Anyone who has opened your app can read and change every row, signed in or not
Rules are switched on, but no policy has been written A list that should have rows is empty Nobody is allowed to read anything yet

The first is dangerous precisely because nothing looks wrong. The second explains a common puzzle. When the rules block a read, the database does not report an error. It returns an empty list. To the app, “you may not see these rows” and “there are no rows” look the same. You met that in session 6.

Worked example. Your app shows each customer only their own orders, because the page asks the database for “orders where the customer is me.” The rules on the orders table are switched off. A customer opens developer tools, removes that filter from the request and downloads every customer’s orders.

The page’s filter was a convenience. A policy on the table would have enforced the restriction.

Rule of thumb: for every table, you should be able to say who may read it and who may change it. If you cannot, nobody has decided.

Test it with two accounts

You find out whether access is enforced by signing in as someone who should be refused and trying anyway.

Reading the rules is not enough. Neither is the agent’s report. This is session 1’s third question again: how will you know it worked?

A good auditor does not read the door policy and go home. The auditor walks the building and tries the handles.

The two-account test takes five minutes.

  1. Create two customer accounts, A and B.
  2. Sign in as A and place an order.
  3. Sign in as B. Try to see A’s order, change it and delete it.
  4. Try three ways. Use the screens. Type the address of A’s order into the address bar. Ask the agent to send the request directly while signed in as B.
  5. Repeat as a customer trying to reach a staff page, and as a customer trying to make themselves staff.
  6. Each time, look at what came back in developer tools, not only at the screen. A hidden column may still have arrived.

If any attempt succeeds, you have found a hole. Have the agent fix it with a policy on the table, then run the test again.

Then make it permanent. In session 5 you learned to ask for tests on what matters. A permission test signs in as one user and confirms that they cannot read or change another user’s data. Ask the agent to write the two-account test as permission tests, so it runs on every pull request.

One detail makes a permission test worthless if it is wrong. The test must sign in as an ordinary user. A test that connects with the secret key, covered next, skips every rule and proves nothing.

Rule of thumb: an access rule is not finished until you have watched it refuse someone.

Keys: public by design, or secret

Some keys are meant to be seen by everyone, and others must never leave your server. The difference decides where each may live.

Session 2 taught you that a secret key stays on the server. Now there are two kinds of key to tell apart.

Kind of key What it can do Where it may live
Public by design Identifies your app to the database. It can do only what your policies allow. In the browser. Every visitor receives it.
Secret Skips every policy, or spends your money at a paid service On the server only

The public key is not a mistake or a leak. It is safe because your policies stand behind it. This is why the first situation in the last section is so dangerous. With the rules switched off, the public key opens everything.

Think of a building again. The street address is public. The master key is not. Knowing the address lets you walk up to the door, where the badge reader decides. The master key opens every door and ignores the badge readers.

When a secret leaks

Secrets leak in ordinary ways. Someone pushes the secrets file to GitHub. Someone pastes a key into a chat to ask for help. The agent places a key in a file that is sent to the browser.

Deleting the key from the file is not enough. Session 3 taught you that Git keeps every earlier version, so the key is still in the history. It may already have been copied.

The fix is to rotate the key. You create a new key at the service that issued it, and you cancel the old one. This is what a building does when a master key goes missing. It changes the locks.

When a key does not work, ask the agent to check its shape, not to show it. The agent can report how long the key is and whether it starts with the expected letters. Anything printed on the screen stays in the conversation.

Rule of thumb: if a secret has been anywhere it should not be, rotate it. Do not try to work out whether anyone saw it.

Data that must not travel

Sending data to another service copies it to that company’s computers, and some data has rules about where it may go.

Your app will hold data that is not yours to share.

  • Personal data: names, email addresses, phone numbers and anything else that identifies a person.
  • Business data from others: a supplier’s price list, a customer’s order history, anything received under a contract or a confidentiality agreement.
  • Credentials: passwords and keys.

The rule that builders most often miss is this: sending data somewhere is copying it there. That includes more places than people expect.

Where data goes Example
A prompt to an AI model “Summarize these customer complaints,” with names attached
A log A line that records the full contents of a sign-up form
An AI coding agent Letting the agent read a file of real customer records
An outside service Sending order details to an email or analytics service

Professionals already live by this. An accountant does not forward one client’s tax return to another firm because it was convenient. Once you send it, a copy sits on someone else’s system, and you cannot take it back.

Before data goes anywhere, ask three questions. Does the person or company it belongs to know? Does the contract allow it? Does it need to go at all?

Five habits follow.

  1. Collect only what the feature needs.
  2. Keep personal data in the database, behind policies, and nowhere else.
  3. Have logs record what happened and an ID, never a person’s details. “Customer 42: order failed” is enough.
  4. Remove names before sending text to an AI model, when the task does not need them.
  5. Use made-up data while you build. Do not put real customer or supplier data into any AI tool until you have checked the contract and the tool’s terms.

The fifth habit applies to your first job. If your employer hands you real customer data, the question of which AI tools may see it is not yours to answer alone.

Rule of thumb: before you paste anything into an AI tool, ask whose data it is.

Run a security review now and then

An AI security review reads your changes for weaknesses you did not think to test.

The two-account test checks the rules you thought of. A security review looks for the ones you missed. Examples are a table with no policy, a secret key in browser code, or a form that lets a user slip in commands.

Claude Code has one built in.

What you want How When to use it
Check your changes for security weaknesses Type /security-review in the message box Before a change to sign-in, access rules, keys or personal data goes live, and every few weeks

The review reports what it found and why each finding matters. Read the findings with the same care as session 5’s code review. Ask the agent to explain any you do not understand, then have it fix the ones that apply. Then run the two-account test again, because a fix is a change too.

Companies hire outside testers once or twice a year for the same reason. The people who built the locks are the ones least likely to spot the open window.

A review is a second line, not the first. It does not replace the two-account test or your own decisions about who may see what.

Rule of thumb: review security before a risky change goes live, and on a schedule even when nothing seems wrong.

Key terms

Term Plain-English meaning
Authentication Proving who a user is
Authorization Deciding what a user may do, to which records
Token A long, signed string the browser receives at sign-in and sends with every request to prove who is asking
Role A kind of user, such as staff or customer, with its own set of permissions
Cost What the shop paid for a product, as opposed to the price it charges
Policy A rule attached to a table that says which rows a request may touch, based on who is asking
Two-account test Signing in as one user and trying to reach another user’s data
Permission test An automated test that confirms one user cannot read or change another user’s data
Public key A key every visitor receives, which can do only what your policies allow
Secret key A key that skips every policy or spends your money, and so stays on the server
Rotate To replace a leaked key with a new one and cancel the old one
Personal data Anything that identifies a person, such as a name, email address or phone number
Security review An AI reading of your changes that looks for security weaknesses
