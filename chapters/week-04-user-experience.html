User Experience: Screens People Can Actually Use — Vibe Programming, Session 4

Adapted and simplified from three of Scott Murff’s chapters, Interface and Flow, Design Fundamentals and Testing the Design, in his textbook Intro to Product Management. The ideas are his. The wording and the business examples were written for these readings.

Reading time: about 20 minutes. Class opens with a short quiz on this reading.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

  1. Map the flow of a task before you ask for any screen.
  2. Check whether a screen has one clear primary action.
  3. Name the four states a screen has besides the one where everything goes right.
  4. Rewrite a vague label or message, and give the agent a precise design instruction.
  5. Run a short usability test by watching people instead of asking them.

The big idea: the agent will not tell you the app is confusing

Your app works. In session 2 you learned where its parts live. In session 3 you learned to change it safely. This session you ask a different question: can a person who has never seen it get their work done?

User experience, or UX, is everything a person goes through while using a product to reach a goal.

An agent is good at making a screen look polished. Colors, fonts and spacing are its strongest skill. But looks are the smallest part of the job. Underneath are harder questions. What steps does the task take? What is on each screen, and what is it called? What does the user think is happening?

An agent answers those questions silently, with whatever it guessed. It will build a working app that nobody can figure out, and it will not tell you.

Here is the main point of the session: every screen should make the next step obvious, and most of what users experience happens when things do not go perfectly. Judging that is your job.

Flows before screens

Map the steps of the task first, then ask for the screens those steps need.

A flow is the sequence of steps a user takes to finish one task. Ask an agent for “a shipping screen” and you will get one, with whatever steps it guessed. The flow is where you decide what the app asks of a person.

A flow has five parts.

Part What it is The question it answers
Goal The one job the user came to do What does “done” look like?
Entry point Where the flow starts How do they arrive?
Steps Each screen or action, in order What is the one thing they do here?
Decisions Points where the path branches What choice is made, and where does each choice lead?
Exits Every way the flow can end Where do they end up, and can they get back?

Event planners do this already. They map a guest’s path from the door to the seat before they order the decorations. A beautiful room that guests cannot find their way through is still wrong.

Worked example. Here is the flow for shipping an order in the example shop.

GOAL: Record that order 2042 shipped.

ENTRY  The list of orders waiting to ship
 1.  Open order 2042
 2.  Click "Ship"
 3.  Enter the quantity packed and the tracking number
 4.  Confirm
       DECISION: did everything on the order ship?
         all of it  -> EXIT: order is closed, stock goes down, and the dashboard updates
         part of it -> EXIT: order stays open, showing what is still owed
 CANCEL at any step before Confirm -> nothing is saved

Writing it out raises questions that a picture of a screen hides. What happens when the customer ordered three pints and only two are in the freezer? What if a package is lost? Can a shipped order be corrected? Each of these is a business decision. If you do not make it, the agent makes it for you.

Count the steps and the decisions. Each one is a place where a busy user can stop or go wrong. Remove steps the user does not care about, and make sure every exit leads somewhere useful.

Rule of thumb: write the flow before you ask for the screen, and paste the flow into your request.

One primary action per screen

Each screen should have one action that stands out, so the user knows what to do without reading everything.

People do not read a screen from top to bottom. They scan it, and their eyes go to whatever stands out. If everything stands out equally, nothing does. The user must read every label to find the next step.

A designer ranks the items on a screen with five tools: size, boldness, color, the empty space around an item, and position. The action most users came for gets the strongest treatment. Everything else is made quieter on purpose.

A grocery store ranks its products the same way. The best sellers sit at eye level. Slower items go on the bottom shelf. Nothing is thrown away. It is ranked.

Worked example. The agent builds the shop’s order screen with five buttons in a row, all the same blue: Ship, Edit, Cancel order, Print packing slip and Email customer. The owner pauses and reads all five. Worse, Cancel order sits right next to Ship, the same size and color.

The fix removes nothing. Ship becomes the one large, filled button. Edit becomes a plain outlined button. Print packing slip and Email customer become small text links. Cancel order moves away from Ship and asks for confirmation.

You can test any screen in ten seconds with the squint test. Squint at the screen, or blur a screenshot, until you cannot read the words. Whatever still stands out is what users will see first. If that is not the primary action, the ranking is wrong.

Rule of thumb: before you squint, write down the one action you want. Then see whether the screen agrees.

Off the happy path

A screen has more states than the one you demonstrated, and the others are where users get stuck.

The happy path is the run through the app where everything goes right. The data exists, the connection is fast and the input is valid. An agent builds the happy path first, because that is what your request described.

Real users spend a lot of time elsewhere. Every screen has four more states, and each needs to be designed on purpose.

State When it happens What a good one does
Empty First use, or nothing to show yet Explains what will appear here and offers one action to start
Loading The app is waiting for data Shows that something is coming
Error Something failed Says what happened in plain words, keeps what the user typed and offers a next step
Partial Some data loaded and some did not Shows what exists and marks what is missing

Each state should answer two questions: what is true right now, and what can I do next?

A customer service team is built around the same fact. The script for a happy customer is one line. Most of the training covers the exceptions: late packages, wrong items, refunds and upset callers.

Worked example. Look at the shop’s dashboard for a product added yesterday. It has no sales yet. An agent-built app often shows a blank area here, or “Days of stock left: 0.” Zero says the product is about to run out, which is false. Nobody has bought it yet. A good empty state says, “No sales yet. Days of stock left appears after the first order.”

Buttons that change data

One case deserves its own rule. Suppose the owner clicks Ship and nothing on the screen changes for two seconds. The owner assumes the click missed and clicks again. The action runs twice. The stock count drops twice, and the dashboard now shows less stock than the shelf holds.

Every button that saves, sends or deletes needs two kinds of feedback. It should change the moment it is pressed. Then it should stay disabled, showing that it is working, until the action finishes.

Rule of thumb: for each main screen, ask the agent to show you all four states with sample data, and look at each one.

Words are interface

Labels, buttons and messages are the instructions for using your app, and specific words beat clever ones.

Every word a user has to puzzle over slows them down. Two rules cover most cases.

  • A button says what happens when you press it. Use a verb and an object. “Ship 3 pints” tells the user more than “Submit.”
  • Use the user’s words, not the database’s. The owner says “days of stock left.” The owner does not say “dsl_calc” or “record.”

A recipe runs on the same discipline. It says “Add 2 cups of flour.” It does not say “Process ingredients.” Vague words in a recipe become ruined cakes.

Error messages matter most. A good one says what happened, why, and what to do next. It does not blame the user, and it does not throw away what they typed.

Worked example. Three rewrites for the example shop.

Before After Why it is better
Button: Submit Ship 3 pints Says what will happen
“Something went wrong.” “This order has already shipped, so it cannot be shipped again. Open the order to see what was sent.” Says what happened, why, and what to do
A blank list “No orders waiting to ship. New orders appear here when customers place them.” Explains the emptiness and offers the next step

Rule of thumb: copy out every button label in your app. Circle each one that does not say what happens.

Sketch first, then direct precisely

Ask for a rough sketch before a polished screen, and tell the agent exactly what to change and why.

Sketch first

Designers describe their drafts by fidelity: how closely a draft matches the finished product. A rough sketch is low fidelity. A mockup, a styled picture of a finished screen, is high fidelity.

High fidelity used to be expensive, so people sketched first to save work. An agent removed the cost. It can produce a polished screen in a minute. That creates a new risk. People react to what they are shown. Show someone a rough sketch, and they comment on whether the steps make sense. Show them a polished screen, and they comment on the colors.

So name your question first, then ask for the cheapest draft that answers it.

Your question Ask the agent for
Are these the right steps and screens? A rough text sketch of each screen, with no code written
What is on each screen, and what is it called? A plain gray-box layout with the real labels
Does it look right and read clearly? A styled mockup
Can a person finish the task without help? A working version to click through

Architects work this way. They show a client a rough floor plan before any drawing of finished rooms. Moving a wall on paper costs nothing. Moving a real one is expensive, and by then everyone argues about the paint.

Direct precisely

“Make it look better” gives the agent no problem to fix. It will change things at random. A useful design instruction has five parts.

Part Example
Where “On the order screen, the row of buttons”
What is wrong “Five equal buttons, so the owner reads all of them before acting”
Why “One primary action per screen”
What to change “Make Ship the only filled button. Outline Edit. Turn Print packing slip and Email customer into text links.”
Reference A screenshot of your screen, or of an app that does it well

Claude Code accepts images, so a screenshot is often the best reference you can give.

Decide once

The agent has no memory between conversations. Left alone, it will style each new screen from scratch, and after ten conversations you will have five styles of button. The fix is the one from session 1. Put your design decisions in your instruction file:

  • One filled button per screen. Everything else is outlined or a text link.
  • Every list has an empty state with one sentence and one action.
  • Every button that saves or deletes shows that it is working and cannot be clicked twice.
  • Deleting or cancelling asks for confirmation.

Rule of thumb: when you catch yourself fixing the same design problem twice, write the rule down.

Watch, do not ask

You find out whether an app is usable by watching people try to use it, not by asking what they think.

You built this app. You know where every button is. A new user does not. This is called the curse of knowledge: once you know something, it is almost impossible to imagine not knowing it. The agent has the same problem. It knows what every element was meant to do, so it cannot stand in for a first-time user.

A usability test means watching a real user try a real task while you stay quiet and take notes. It is not a demonstration, and it is not a survey.

Lean calls this going to the gemba, the place where the work happens. A manager who wants to know how a process really runs does not read the procedure or ask in a meeting. The manager goes to the floor and watches.

Four rules make a test work.

  1. Use about five people. The first tester shows you a lot. Later testers mostly repeat the same problems. So test five, fix the worst problem and test again with new people.
  2. Give a goal, not instructions. Say, “A customer ordered three pints of Honey Oat Swirl, but only two are in the freezer. Ship what you can.” Do not say, “Click Ship.”
  3. Stay silent. Do not help, explain or defend. Let them struggle. Ask them to think out loud.
  4. Record what they did before what they said. A tester who says “this is easy” after three wrong clicks has told you two things. The clicks are the reliable one.

Worked example. You watch five classmates try the Honey Oat Swirl task. Four of them type 2, click Ship and then look for a way to say that one pint is still owed. The app has closed the order. Nobody complained. All four said the app was “pretty clear.” You would never have found the problem by asking.

Afterward, rank what you saw by how badly it blocks the task. A problem that stops users from finishing comes first. A dated-looking icon comes last. Fix the top one or two, then test again, because a fix can fail or create a new problem.

Classmates are good practice, and they will find where a newcomer gets confused. They are not the people who pack orders every day. Before you trust a result, test with people who do the real job.

Rule of thumb: never ask “do you like it?” Ask them to do something, and watch.

Key terms

Term Plain-English meaning
User experience (UX) Everything a person goes through while using a product to reach a goal
Flow The sequence of steps a user takes to finish one task
Primary action The one action on a screen that most users came to take
Squint test Blurring a screen until the words are unreadable, to see what stands out
Happy path The run through the app where everything goes right
Empty, loading, error and partial states What a screen shows when there is no data, when it is waiting, when something failed and when only some data arrived
Fidelity How closely a draft matches the finished product
Mockup A styled picture of a finished screen
Curse of knowledge Once you know something, it is almost impossible to imagine not knowing it
Usability test Watching a real user try a real task while you stay quiet and take notes
