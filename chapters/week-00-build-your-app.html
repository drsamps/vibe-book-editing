Build and Publish Your App — Vibe Programming, Session 0

Adapted and simplified from Scott Murff’s chapter Building with AI Agents, in his textbook Intro to Product Management. The ideas are his. The wording and the business examples were written for these readings.

Reading time: about 20 minutes. Class opens with a short quiz on this reading.

Learning objectives

By the end of this session you can:

  1. Explain what vibe programming is, and the standard this course holds it to.
  2. Explain the difference between a chatbot and an agent.
  3. Name the three phases of the agent loop, and use them to say why a result went wrong.
  4. Describe your own part in the loop: briefing, approving, interrupting and checking.
  5. Describe the app you will build, and explain why the course has you build it before you understand it.

The big idea: build first, understand after

At the start of the course you will build a working business app and publish it on the internet. It should take perhaps an hour, in plain English, without writing a line of code. If you get stuck, the TAs and your instructor can help you finish.

Then you will spend ten more sessions finding out what you built.

That order is deliberate. Here is the main point of this reading: an AI agent can build a working app faster than you can understand it. So you build first, then take the app apart, one layer per session.

You cannot learn everything before you build anything. The fastest way to understand how software fits together is to have some of your own running, and to study that.

You will not become a programmer in this course. You will become a manager who can direct an AI agent, check its work and know when not to trust it. That skill is useful in any business job where software gets built, bought or changed.

What vibe programming is

Vibe programming means describing what you want in plain language and letting an AI write the code.

The name comes from Andrej Karpathy, a founding member of OpenAI. In February 2025 he described a new way of building software that he called “vibe coding.” You tell the AI what you want, it writes the code, and you barely look at the code at all. The phrase spread quickly. Collins Dictionary made it the word of the year for 2025.

People disagree about whether this is a good idea.

  • Skeptics say it produces builders who do not understand their own systems. That leads to more errors and to confusion about who is responsible when something breaks.
  • Supporters say it is the next step in a long trend. Each generation of programming tools has let people work further from the machine’s own language and closer to their own intent.

Both sides are partly right. This course is built on the overlap: let the agent write the code, and keep the understanding for yourself.

That is a real standard. Careless AI-built software has a name, “AI slop,” and some engineers now make a living cleaning it up. You do not want to be the reason your company hires one.

Business has seen this before. Outsourcing work does not outsource responsibility. A company that hires an outside firm to run its payroll still answers to its employees when a paycheck is wrong. You are outsourcing the typing. You still own the result.

Rule of thumb: if you cannot say what a part of your app does, you are not finished with it.

A chatbot answers. An agent acts.

An agent is an AI model that works in a loop with tools, so it can take actions and see their results.

You have probably used a chatbot such as ChatGPT or Claude. You ask a question, and it writes an answer. The AI part is called a model: a program that takes in text and images and writes text back. Writing text is all a model can do.

An agent is a model that has been given tools. A tool is one action the model is allowed to ask for, such as “read this file,” “change this file” or “run this command.” A program around the model carries out each request and shows the model what happened. Then the model decides what to do next.

The difference matters in practice.

A chatbot An agent
You ask for a sales dashboard It describes one, or writes code for you to copy It creates the files, runs the app and checks that it starts
Where it works In a chat window On your computer, in your project
What can go wrong A wrong answer A wrong action

The agent you will use is Claude Code, made by the company Anthropic. It can read the files in your project, change them and run commands on your computer.

This course uses Claude Code in the Claude desktop app, on its Code tab. You type in a message box and click buttons, as in any other app. The same agent also runs in a terminal, a typing-only window described below. If you prefer the terminal, you can use it. The readings point out the few places where it works differently.

Think of two people at a retail chain. An analyst can study last month’s sales and recommend a price cut. A store manager with access to the pricing system can make the change. The analyst is the chatbot. The manager is the agent. The difference is the access.

That last row of the table is the reason for session 1. An agent with access can do real work, and it can do real damage.

Rule of thumb: a chatbot gives you words to check. An agent gives you actions to check.

The agent loop: gather context, act, check

Every piece of work the agent does goes through three phases, and a bad result usually traces to one of them.

Phase What the agent does What goes wrong here
Gather context Reads your files and your instructions It never looked at the file that mattered
Act Changes something It changed the wrong thing, or changed too much
Check Looks at the result of its action It skipped the check, or the check could not see the problem

The loop repeats until the task is done or you stop it.

You may already know a loop like this. Quality management teaches plan, do, check, act (PDCA). You study the situation, make a change, check the result and adjust. The agent runs a version of that cycle many times a minute.

Worked example. You tell the agent, “The Delete button on my product list does not work.” A good run looks like this.

  1. Gather context. It reads the file for the product screen and finds the code behind the Delete button.
  2. Act. It sees that the code removes the product from the screen but never saves the change. It adds the save.
  3. Check. It runs the app, deletes a test product, reloads the page and confirms the product is gone.

Now three bad runs, one for each phase.

  • It “fixes” a different button, because it never opened the product screen. That is a context failure.
  • It rewrites the whole screen and breaks the Add button. That is an action failure.
  • It announces “Fixed!” without ever reloading the page. That is a check failure.

The loop gives you a way to diagnose the agent. When a result is wrong, ask which phase failed. Each has a different fix.

What failed What you do
Context Point it to the right file, or tell it the rule it was missing
Action Make the request smaller and more specific
Check Tell it how to verify the result, or verify it yourself

Rule of thumb: when the agent gets it wrong, ask which phase failed before you ask it to try again.

You are part of the loop

The agent does the typing. You brief it, approve its actions, interrupt it and check its work.

Picture the agent as a fast, capable new hire with no memory, who takes you literally. That picture tells you your four jobs.

  • Brief it. The agent knows only what is in front of it. It does not know your business, your customers or what you meant but did not say.
  • Approve. Before some actions, Claude Code stops and asks your permission. Read what it is asking before you say yes. Session 1 covers how to decide what it may do without asking.
  • Interrupt. You can stop the agent at any moment by clicking the stop button or pressing the Esc key. If it is heading the wrong way, stop it and redirect. Do not wait for it to finish.
  • Check. The agent will tell you it is done. That is a claim. Open the app and see for yourself.

One part of the new-hire comparison breaks, and it is important. A new hire learns from yesterday. The agent does not, unless the lesson is written down where it will read it. Session 1 shows you where.

You will see some new things on the screen. Two words will help.

  • A terminal is a window where you control a computer by typing instead of clicking.
  • A command is one typed instruction, such as an instruction to delete a file.

You will not memorize commands. The agent types them, and the desktop app shows each one in the conversation. You only need to recognize that a command is an action, and that some actions are hard to undo. A deleted file does not go to the trash.

A shift supervisor has the same job. The supervisor does not do every task. The supervisor sets up the work, approves the first result, steps in when something looks wrong and inspects the output.

Rule of thumb: read before you approve, and look before you believe.

What you will build in class

You will build a small business app of your own and publish it at a web address. Your instructor approves the idea first.

The approval is there to make sure your app has enough to it. It needs to be worth taking apart for the rest of the course. A useful shape is an app that keeps records of a few related kinds of things and calculates something a manager would act on.

The readings use one example app throughout: The Dreamery, “where sweet dreams come true.” It is a made-up campus creamery, a business that makes and sells ice cream, and it also bakes cookies. Customers buy pints and half-gallons of ice cream and boxes of cookies at the shop on campus. They can also order online and have them shipped. The Dreamery and all of its numbers are invented for these readings. If you are curious how a real campus creamery’s supply chain works, from the dairy to the freezer case, read the Marriott School’s article “Frozen Assets”.

In its first version the example shop has four parts.

Part What it holds
Products What the shop sells, with a price and the number in stock
Customers Who buys from the shop
Orders Who bought what, how many, when, and whether it has shipped
Sales dashboard Numbers calculated from the order history

A dashboard is a screen that sums up records as a few numbers a manager can act on. The shop’s dashboard shows the best sellers and how many of each product sold per week. It also shows how many days of stock are left at that pace, and it flags any product that is running low. The owner uses it to decide what to reorder and what to stop carrying.

Your own app should have a part like this: something it calculates from the records it keeps. A club’s event app might calculate attendance trends. A tutoring service might calculate hours per tutor.

Each of you builds your own app. Do not reuse a team project from another course, because every student needs an app that is theirs to change and to break.

Two words describe where your app runs.

  • While you build, the app runs on your own computer, and only you can see it.
  • To publish an app is to put it on a computer that anyone can reach over the internet. The published copy is called the live app, and it has its own web address.

Once your app is published, you will open it on your phone.

You will sign up for a few services before class: one for the AI agent, one to store your project, one to store your data and one to publish the app. Your instructor will send the setup steps. Do them before class, because setup is slow and class time is short.

Rule of thumb: start small. An app that does four things correctly is worth more than one that does twelve things badly.

Why build before you understand

Building an app in about an hour will make you feel that you understand it. You will not, yet, and knowing that is the starting point of the course.

Psychologists Justin Kruger and David Dunning described a pattern in 1999 that now carries their names. People who are new to a skill often rate their own ability too high. They do not yet know enough to see what they are missing. This is called the Dunning-Kruger effect.

Vibe programming is a perfect setup for it. After about an hour you will have a working, published app. It will look finished. You will be tempted to conclude that building software is easy.

Then ask yourself a few questions.

  • Where is your app’s data stored, and who can read it?
  • If a number your app calculates is wrong, where would you look?
  • What stops a stranger from changing your records?
  • What will the app cost to run if a thousand people use it?
  • If the agent broke it tomorrow, how would you get yesterday’s version back?

You will not be able to answer most of them. That is expected. Each one is a session of this course.

A store visit works the same way. After an hour in the aisles, a visitor feels they understand the business. The store manager knows about the late deliveries, the theft, the slow sellers and the register that freezes on busy days. The course moves you from the visitor’s view toward the manager’s.

Engineers learn a competitor’s product by doing a teardown. They take it apart, one layer at a time, and ask why each part is there. That is the plan for the next ten sessions, applied to your own app.

Session What you take apart
1 Directing the agent: what it can see, what it may do, how you check it
2 Architecture: the parts of an app and how they fit
3 GitHub: save points, review and the human in the loop
4 User experience: screens people can actually use
5 Testing: how you know it works
6 Databases: where the data lives
7 Logins and security: who may do what
8 Deployment: publishing, and undoing a bad publish
9 Connections: reaching other systems
10 AI in your app: models and what they cost

Each session follows the same rhythm. You find that session’s layer in your own app. You learn the idea in plain English. You direct the agent to change it. You break something and fix it. Then you discuss what it means for a business.

Rule of thumb: treat a feeling of “this is easy” as a sign that the Dunning-Kruger effect may be at work, and ask one more question.
